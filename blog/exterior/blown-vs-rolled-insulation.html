Blown-In vs. Rolled Insulation: Which is Actually Cheaper?

Field Notes

  • The "Void" Rule: Why a 5% gap in your insulation drops the R-value by 50%.
  • R-Value per Dollar: Blown-in cellulose is cheaper materially, but requires a machine rental.
  • The "Settling" Factor: Fiberglass stays fluffy; cellulose settles over time. Here is how to over-fill to compensate.

90% of US homes are under-insulated. If your home was built before 2000, you are likely burning cash every winter. Upgrading your attic from R-19 (about 6 inches deep) to R-49 (about 16 inches deep) can lower your energy bill by 20% immediately.

But when you walk into the hardware store, you face a choice: Pink Rolls (Fiberglass) or Bags of Fluff (Cellulose) ?

Most DIYers grab the rolls because they look easy. "Just unroll it like a sleeping bag," they think. In reality, cutting rolls to fit perfectly around thousands of nails, wires, and junction boxes is a nightmare. And in insulation, fit is everything .

The Safety Gear

Attics are dangerous. It is hot, dark, and full of glass fibers that want to get into your lungs. Do not go up there without this kit.

  • N95 Respirator Fiberglass dust is brutal. A simple paper mask isn't enough. Get a valved N95.
  • LED Headlamp You need both hands free to cut or hold the hose. Don't use a flashlight.
  • Insulation Calculator Calculate exactly how many bags or rolls you need for R-49 or R-60.

The Tale of the Tape: Batts vs. Blow

When you look at the price tag on the shelf, Fiberglass seems cheaper. But you aren't paying for "stuff," you are paying for R-Value (Thermal Resistance). When you do the math on R-value per dollar, the winner changes.

Factor Fiberglass Batts (Pink) Blown-In Cellulose (Grey)
Composition Spun glass fibers (Itchy) Recycled paper + Borate (Dusty)

Why Batts Fail in Attics

Imagine trying to fit a square sleeping bag into a round hole. That is what installing Batts is like.

Your attic floor is not a flat surface. It is a minefield of joists, wires, recessed light cans, and PVC pipes .

"The Void Rule": If your insulation has just a 5% gap (due to poor cutting around a pipe), the effective R-value of that entire wall drops by 50%.

Is Fiberglass Ever the Right Choice?

Yes: If you are insulating open wall cavities (vertical walls) during a renovation before the drywall goes up. Batts stay in place vertically thanks to friction.

Step 0: The "Wool Sweater" Rule

Adding insulation without Air Sealing first is like wearing a thick wool sweater with the window open. The sweater keeps you warm, but the wind cuts right through it. Insulation works the same way: it stops heat transfer, but it does not stop air movement.

1. Buy the "Great Stuff" First

Before you rent the blower machine, go into your attic with 5 cans of expanding spray foam. Move the old insulation aside and look for black marks on the existing fiberglass. That black dust is a filter mark—it means air is leaking from your house into the attic right there.

ACTION: Seal around every wire hole, plumbing pipe, and the tops of interior wall plates.

2. Don't Choke Your Roof

Your roof needs to breathe. Air enters through the "soffit" vents under the eaves. If you blow insulation all the way to the edge, you will block these vents. This causes ice dams in winter and cooks your shingles in summer.

ACTION: Staple pink styrofoam "baffles" (vents) between the rafters before you blow.

3. The Fire Hazard

Old recessed light cans get hot. If you bury a non-rated light in cellulose (which is paper), you risk a fire. Check your lights from the attic side.

RULE: If the can says "IC-Rated" (Insulation Contact), you can bury it. If not, you must build a box around it to keep insulation 3 inches away.

Insulation FAQ: Questions I Get at the Yard

Can I blow new insulation over the old stuff?
**Yes.** In fact, it's recommended. Unless the old insulation is wet, moldy, or full of rodent droppings, leave it there. It still has R-value. Just blow the cellulose right on top of it to bring the total depth up to 15-18 inches.
Do I really need a vapor barrier?
If you are adding to an existing attic, **NO**. You never want to sandwich a vapor barrier between two layers of insulation—that traps moisture and causes rot. The painted drywall on your ceiling acts as your vapor barrier. Just blow the insulation directly onto the attic floor.
Is cellulose flammable? It's just paper, right?
It is treated with **Borate**, a fire retardant. In testing, cellulose actually slows down fire better than fiberglass because it packs so densely that it blocks the oxygen fire needs to burn. It is Class 1 Fire Rated.

The Verdict

If you are building a new wall, buy the rolls. But if you are upgrading an attic, rent the machine and blow it in. It is cheaper, faster, and covers the gaps that rob your home of heat. Just don't forget the air sealing first.
