How to Cut Stair Stringers: The "Measure Twice" Guide

Safety Briefing

  • The "Trip" Tolerance: Building code says the difference between your tallest and shortest step can be no more than 3/8". Precision matters.
  • The "Throat" Danger: Never leave less than 5 inches of wood uncut on the stringer, or it becomes structural kindling.
  • Pressure Treated Only: Stringers touch the ground and rot quickly. Use 2x12 Ground Contact lumber.

I’ve seen more botched stairs than I can count. I’ve walked on decks where the bottom step was 4 inches high and the top step was 9 inches high. You know what happens? You trip. Every single time.

Our brains are wired for rhythm. When we climb stairs, we expect every step to be exactly the same height. If one step is off by even half an inch, your foot catches the edge and down you go.

Cutting stringers (the sawtooth support boards) is the single hardest part of deck building. It requires grade-school math, a steady hand, and a $5 tool called a "Stair Gauge" that most DIYers forget to buy.

Put away the circular saw for a minute. We are going to do the math first.

The Layout Kit

You can't eyeball this. You need precise layout tools.

  • Steel Framing Square The big "L" shaped ruler. Essential for marking the angles.
  • Brass Stair Gauges Small brass knobs that clamp onto your square. They lock in your rise and run so you don't make mistakes.
  • Stair Calculator Don't do long division on scrap wood. Get your exact Rise and Run here.

Step 1: Know Your Numbers

Before we cut wood, we need two numbers. Get your tape measure and a helper.

Total Rise

The vertical distance from the top of the finished deck floor down to the landing pad (concrete or patio stones) where the stairs will end.

Example: 35 inches

Unit Run

How deep each step is. This is where your foot lands. I never build a stair with less than a 10-inch run . Anything less feels like climbing a ladder.

Standard: 10 inches

Step 2: Calculate the "Unit Rise"

Building code (IRC) dictates that the maximum height of a single step is usually 7 ¾ inches . You can be lower, but you can't be higher.

The 3-Step Math

1. Find Number of Steps (Divide Total Rise by 7.75)

35" ÷ 7.75 = 4.51

2. Round UP to the nearest whole number

4.51 → 5 Steps

3. Find Exact Rise (Divide Total Rise by Steps)

35" ÷ 5 = 7 inches

Result: You will cut 5 steps, and every single one must be exactly 7 inches high.

Step 3: Trust the Brass

Do not try to freehand this with a tape measure. You need your Framing Square and those Brass Gauges (stair nuts).

1

Set the Gauges

Clamp one gauge on the outer edge of the square at your Rise number (e.g., 7 inches). Clamp the other gauge at your Run number (e.g., 10 inches).

2

Step and Repeat

Hook the gauges onto the edge of your 2x12 pressure-treated board. Mark the triangle. Slide the square down so it aligns perfectly with your previous mark. Repeat this 5 times (or however many steps you calculated).

Step 4: Drop the Stringer

The "One Inch" Rule

If you cut the bottom step the same height as the others, YOU WILL FAIL INSPECTION.

Why? You are going to add a tread (deck board) on top of the stringer. That adds 1 inch of height.

If your rise is 7", adding a tread makes the bottom step 8" (Too High). It also pushes the whole stair up, making the top step only 6" (Too Low).

THE FIX: You must cut the thickness of your tread (usually 1") off the very bottom of the stringer.

Step 5: Check the "Throat"

The triangular notches you cut out are waste. The wood that is left underneath is the structure. This is called the Throat .

If your throat is less than 5 inches , the stringer is too weak. It will snap when someone carries a heavy cooler up the stairs.

Use 2x12 lumber (not 2x10) to ensure a safe throat depth.

Stair FAQ: Passing Inspection

How do I attach the stringer to the deck?
**Do not just toe-nail it.** Nails pull out. You must use metal "Stair Stringer Hangers" (like Simpson Strong-Tie LSCZ). They bend to match the angle of your stairs and lag screw into the rim joist. It's the only way to sleep at night.
Do I need a concrete pad at the bottom?
**Yes.** You cannot rest stairs on dirt. The wood will rot and the stairs will sink, messing up your "Rise" calculation. Pour a small 4-inch thick concrete pad or use heavy 24"x24" patio pavers on a compacted gravel base.
Open risers or closed risers?
If the stairs are high (more than 30 inches off the ground), code usually requires **Closed Risers** (or risers with gaps smaller than 4 inches). This is to prevent a small child from slipping through the gap and falling.

Cut Once, Cut Right

Stair building separates the carpenters from the wood butchers. Take the time to calculate your exact Unit Rise, use the brass gauges to mark your stringer, and don't forget to drop that bottom cut. Your family's safety depends on that math.
