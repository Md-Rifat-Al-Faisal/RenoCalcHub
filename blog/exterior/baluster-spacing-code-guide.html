Baluster Spacing Code: The 4-Inch Sphere Rule

Code Briefing (IRC R312)

  • The 4-Inch Rule: The gap between balusters (spindles) must be small enough that a 4-inch sphere cannot pass through.
  • The Stair Exception: On the side of stairs, the triangular gap formed by the riser and tread allows for a 6-inch sphere.
  • The Strength Test: The railing must withstand 200 lbs of force pushing horizontally against it.

You spend $15,000 on materials. You labor for three weekends. You stand back and admire your beautiful mahogany deck. Then the building inspector arrives, pulls a red plastic ball out of his truck, pushes it between your spindles, and says two words: "Tear it down."

Baluster spacing isn't an aesthetic choice; it is a life-safety issue. The code isn't arbitrary. It is written in response to accidents. Small children can slide their bodies through a 5-inch gap, but their heads (which are larger than their bodies) get stuck. That is a trap.

In my line of work, we don't eyeball spacing. We calculate it down to the decimal. Today, I'm going to teach you how to do the math so your railing is safe, symmetrical, and passes inspection on the first try.

The Two Ways to Measure

There are two languages in carpentry. You need to know which one you are speaking, or your layout will be wrong.

1. The "Clear Gap" Method

This measures the empty air between two spindles.

Code Rule: MUST be less than 4 inches.

*This is what the inspector checks.*

2. The "On-Center" Method

This measures from the center of one spindle to the center of the next.

Layout Rule: Use this for marking tape.

*This is what YOU use to build.*

How to Calculate Even Spacing

Do not just space them 4 inches apart and leave a random gap at the end. That looks amateur. Here is how to make them perfectly symmetrical.

1
Measure Total Length

Measure the daylight opening between your two posts (e.g., 70 inches).

2
Add One Baluster Width

Take your baluster width (usually 1.5") and add it to the maximum allowable gap (4"). Total unit = 5.5".

3
Divide & Round Up

Divide Length (70) by Unit (5.5) = 12.72.
Round UP to the next whole number: 13 spaces.

4
Find Exact Spacing

Take Length (70) ÷ Spaces (13) = 5.38 inches On-Center.
Subtract the baluster width (1.5") to check gap: 5.38 - 1.5 = 3.88 inches gap.

PASS: 3.88" is less than 4". Perfect.

The Stair Exception (4 ⅜" & 6")

Stairs are geometrically awkward. Because of this, the International Residential Code (IRC) gives you two "bonus" measurements that apply only to stairs.

The Main Gap

4 ⅜"

On flat decks, the limit is 4 inches. On stairs, the code allows a sphere up to 4 ⅜ inches to pass through.

Jack's Advice: Stick to 4" anyway. Why risk it?

The "Triangle"

6"

The triangular gap formed by the stair tread, the riser, and the bottom rail. This can pass a 6-inch sphere .

The Pro Installation Trick

Trying to install balusters on stairs one by one is a nightmare. Gravity fights you, and it's hard to keep them plumb (vertical).

Build It On The Ground

1. Cut your top and bottom rails to the correct angle.
2. Lay them on a flat surface (garage floor).
3. Mark your spacing on both rails simultaneously.
4. Screw the balusters in while flat.
5. Lift the entire "panel" into place and attach it to the posts.

Why Your Math Might Fail

Remember high school geometry? On stairs, the rail length (diagonal) is longer than the horizontal run .

The Warning: If you calculate your spacing based on the horizontal distance, your gaps will be too wide when measured on the diagonal rail. Always measure the actual length of the rail itself to do your division.

Baluster FAQ: The Safety Checks

Can I use horizontal cable railings?
Proceed with caution. While the national code (IRC) technically allows them, many local inspectors hate them because of the "Ladder Effect." Horizontal cables make it easy for toddlers to climb the railing. Always check your local municipality amendments before buying expensive cable hardware.
Does "Pressure Treated" wood shrink?
Yes, massively. A wet PT board can shrink by 1/8" to 1/4" in width as it dries. If you space your balusters at exactly 4.0 inches, in two months that gap might be 4.25 inches—and you technically fail code.
Jack's Rule: Aim for a **3.5-inch gap**. It leaves room for error and shrinkage.
Do I need balusters on a low deck?
If the deck floor is less than **30 inches** above the ground, code generally does not require a railing at all. However, if you choose to install one, it must meet all the spacing rules. You can't build a "half-safe" railing.

The Verdict

The 4-inch rule is not a suggestion; it is the line between a safe deck and a lawsuit. Do the math, space them evenly, and aim for 3.5 inches to be safe. When the inspector pulls out that red ball, you want to be smiling, not sweating.
