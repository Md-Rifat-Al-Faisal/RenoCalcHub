Here is the mistake I see on 90% of DIY job sites: A homeowner measures the square footage of their room, drives to the store, and loads up a stack of 4x8 drywall sheets because they fit nicely in the back of a minivan.

Congratulations. You just doubled your work.

In the professional world, we don't estimate drywall by square footage alone; we estimate by layout efficiency . Every seam you create is a potential crack down the road and 20 minutes of taping and sanding time right now. My goal is to minimize joints, eliminate "butts" (where two non-tapered ends meet), and get you out of the sanding dust faster.

The Gravity Rule: Horizontal vs. Vertical

In commercial steel-stud framing, we hang sheets vertically. But in your wood-framed house? **Hang them horizontally.**

Here is the physics: Wood studs warp and twist over time. If you hang vertically, your seam runs parallel to a single stud for 8 feet. If that stud twists, your entire seam pops. When you hang horizontally, you bridge across 4 to 6 studs. This ties the wall together structurally, making it 30% stronger and hiding minor framing imperfections.

3 Ways to Ruin a Wall (And How to Fix Them)

Drywall is unforgiving. If you screw up the framing or the hanging, no amount of mud will hide it. I’ve fired guys for making these exact mistakes because they cost me days in labor to fix.

1. The "Butt Joint" Nightmare

Drywall sheets have "factory edges" that are tapered (thinner) to accept tape and mud. When you cut a sheet, that edge is full thickness. If you butt two cut edges together, the tape sits on top of the surface, creating a hump.

THE FIX: Avoid butt joints at all costs by using 12-foot sheets. If you must have one, "float" the joint out 12-18 inches wide on each side to hide the hump.

2. Using Mesh Tape with "Bucket Mud"

Self-adhesive mesh tape is convenient, but it is weak. If you cover it with standard pre-mixed "bucket mud" (which shrinks as it dries), the joint will crack .

Rule: If you use Mesh Tape, you MUST use "Hot Mud" (Setting-Type Compound) for the first coat. It hardens chemically and locks the mesh in place.

3. "Popping" the Paper

If you drive a screw too deep and it tears through the paper face of the drywall, that screw is holding nothing . It has zero structural integrity. You must back it out and drive a new screw two inches away. Get a "dimpler" bit for your drill—it stops the screw automatically at the perfect depth.

Jack's "Click" Secret

"Listen to your gun. When a screw hits a stud solid, it makes a distinct 'thud-zip' sound. If it spins freely or sounds high-pitched, you missed the stud. Don't leave it. A missed screw is a future hole in your wall."

Drywall FAQ: The Stuff Nobody Tells You

Should I use 1/2-inch or 5/8-inch drywall?
**Standard walls take 1/2-inch.** However, you must use **5/8-inch (Type X)** for any wall attached to a garage or furnace room (it's fire-rated). I also recommend 5/8-inch for ceilings if your joists are 24-inches apart, to prevent sagging over time.
How much mud do I actually need?
A 4.5-gallon bucket of lightweight all-purpose compound covers about **400-500 square feet** of wall area (roughly 8 to 10 sheets). Do not buy the small tubs; you will run out instantly. Always buy the box or bucket.
Do I really need to glue the sheets?
**Yes.** Applying a bead of construction adhesive to the studs before hanging reduces the number of screws you need and drastically reduces "screw pops" later. It bonds the sheet to the frame, making the wall feel solid rather than hollow.

Do the Math Before You Buy

Drywall is cheap, but labor is expensive. Saving $10 on sheets by buying 8-footers instead of 12-footers will cost you $200 in your time trying to hide extra seams. Measure your room, calculate the staggered layout, and buy the big sheets.
