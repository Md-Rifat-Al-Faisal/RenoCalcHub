AC BTU Sizing Guide: Why "Bigger" Is Not Better

Comfort Notes

  • The "Short Cycle" Trap: Why buying an AC that is too powerful will actually ruin your sleep.
  • Ceiling Height Math: Standard charts assume 8-foot ceilings. Here is how to adjust for high ceilings.
  • Sunlight Factor: A south-facing window can increase your cooling load by 20%.

When summer hits, the instinct is to buy the biggest, most powerful air conditioner you can afford. You want the room cold, and you want it cold now .

But in the world of HVAC, bigger is absolutely not better. It is actually worse.

An air conditioner has two jobs: cooling the air and removing humidity . If your unit is too big (too many BTUs) for the room, it will cool the air down in 5 minutes and shut off. This is called "short cycling." The problem? It didn't run long enough to pull the moisture out of the air. You end up with a room that feels cold, damp, and "clammy"—the perfect environment for mold growth.

We need to find the "Goldilocks" zone: a unit just big enough to keep up with the heat, but small enough that it runs constantly on hot days to keep the air dry and crisp.

Step 1: The "Base" Number

The Department of Energy suggests a baseline of **20 BTUs per square foot** for a standard room with 8-foot ceilings. This gives you a starting point before we add the "load factors."

Here is what that looks like for common room sizes:

Room Size Square Footage Base BTU Needed
Small Bedroom / Office 100 - 150 sq. ft. 5,000 BTU
Standard Bedroom 150 - 250 sq. ft. 6,000 - 7,000 BTU
Master Bedroom / Living 300 - 400 sq. ft. 8,000 - 10,000 BTU
Open Plan Living / Dining 450 - 550 sq. ft. 12,000 - 14,000 BTU
*Assumes standard 8-ft ceilings and moderate insulation.

Step 2: The "Load" Adjustments

This is where most people get it wrong. A 200 sq. ft. bedroom in a basement needs very different cooling than a 200 sq. ft. kitchen with a skylight. You must add (or subtract) based on these factors:

Sunlight Exposure

Windows are heat magnets.

  • Heavily Shaded Room - 10% BTU
  • Sunny (West/South) + 10% BTU

The Kitchen Factor

Ovens and fridges generate massive heat.

Add Flat Rate + 4,000 BTU

High Ceilings

Standard charts assume 8ft height.

For ceilings over 8 feet, calculate the Cubic Footage (L x W x H) instead of square footage.

Rough Rule: Add 10% for every foot over 8ft.

Occupancy

Bodies radiate heat (about 98.6°F).

Per Person (Over 2 people) + 600 BTU

Window vs. Portable vs. Split: The Efficiency Reality

Not all 12,000 BTU units are created equal. A window unit will freeze you out, while a portable unit with the same rating might barely keep you sweating. It comes down to physics.

Window Unit

The Efficiency King

Because the hot motor hangs outside the window, the heat is rejected directly into the outdoors. They seal the room effectively and are the cheapest to run.

High Efficiency Loud
Ductless Mini-Split

The Gold Standard

Whisper quiet because the compressor is outside on the ground. They require a 3-inch hole in the wall but offer the best cooling and heating performance money can buy.

Max Efficiency Expensive Install
Portable (1-Hose)

The "Last Resort"

Avoid these if possible. They sit inside the room. They pump air out the window to exhaust heat, which creates negative pressure .

Low Efficiency Easy Setup

Why Portable Units Fail

Imagine a vacuum cleaner. A single-hose portable AC sucks air from your room, heats it up, and blasts it out the window.

The problem? That air has to be replaced. Physics dictates that for every cubic foot of air pushed out, one cubic foot must come in. So, the unit sucks hot, humid outdoor air into your house through the cracks under your doors and windows. You are cooling the room while simultaneously vacuuming hot air into it.

If you MUST use a portable unit, buy a "Dual Hose" model. One hose pulls outside air in to cool the motor, and the other pushes it back out.

A single hose unit creates a vacuum effect in your room.

AC FAQ: Decoding the Box

What is "Inverter" Technology?
**Buy this if you can.** Old ACs are like a light switch: On (100%) or Off (0%). This creates temperature swings. An "Inverter" is like a dimmer switch. It can run at 20%, 50%, or 100% power to maintain a perfectly steady temperature. It is quieter and uses 30-50% less electricity.
What is SEER vs. EER?
**EER (Energy Efficiency Ratio)** measures efficiency at peak heat (95°F). Look for this if you live in Arizona or Texas.

**SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio)** measures efficiency over a whole season. Look for this in mixed climates. The higher the number, the lower your electric bill. Aim for SEER 15+.
What is "Dry Mode"?
This mode runs the compressor at a low speed specifically to pull moisture out of the air without freezing the room. It's perfect for sticky, rainy days when it's not actually that hot outside (70°F but 90% humidity).

Comfort Over Cold

Don't fall for the trap of buying the biggest machine on the shelf. A properly sized, smaller unit will run longer, dehumidify better, and last longer than an oversized beast that short-cycles all day. Measure your room, account for the sun, and buy smart.
