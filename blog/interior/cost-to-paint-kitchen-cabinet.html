The Essential Math of Cabinet Painting

When I walk into a kitchen, I don't look at the walls—I count the units. A professional estimate is built on the number of openings . A standard 10x10 kitchen typically has **20 to 25 openings**.

For materials, the rule of thumb is: **one gallon of primer and two gallons of high-performance topcoat** will cover roughly **30 openings** (front and back). However, you must factor in the "box" surface area (the frames) and the "end panels."

5 Costly Mistakes Beginners Make

I’ve walked into dozens of kitchens where the "weekend project" went horribly wrong. Kitchen cabinets are unforgiving; any shortcut you take in the preparation phase will show up in the final finish. Avoid these common traps:

1. The "Paint-and-Primer-in-One" Lie

Marketing labels on retail cans are designed for walls, not wood. Cabinets contain tannins and oils that will bleed through standard paint within weeks. You must use a dedicated bonding primer (shellac or oil-based) to create a chemical bond that paint-in-one products simply cannot achieve.

2. Inadequate Degreasing

Even if your cabinets look clean, 20 years of cooking grease has atomized onto the surface. If you don't use a heavy-duty degreaser like TSP , your primer will "fish-eye" or peel off in sheets.

Pro Tip: Focus on the areas around handles and above the stove; these are the highest contamination zones.

3. Ignoring the "Oak Grain" Problem

If you have oak cabinets, simply painting them won't make them look modern. The deep, porous grain will show through the paint like tiny cracks. To get a smooth finish, you must apply a grain filler or two heavy coats of high-build primer followed by aggressive sanding.

4. Reinstalling Hardware Too Early

Modern cabinet enamels take 2-4 hours to dry, but **7 to 14 days** to fully cure. If you put the doors back on and tighten the hinges too early, the paint will "block" (stick together), and the first time you open the door, it will rip the finish right off the frame.

The "Hidden" Waste Factor

When spraying cabinets, your waste factor is much higher than when rolling walls. Between "overspray" and the paint lost in the sprayer lines, you should factor in a 20% waste margin for materials. It’s better to have a quart left for touch-ups than to run out during the final door.

Project FAQ

Can I paint Thermofoil or Laminate cabinets?
**Yes, but with a catch.** If the Thermofoil is peeling or bubbling, you must heat it with a heat gun, peel it off entirely, and paint the MDF core underneath. If it is fully intact, you must use an extreme-bond primer like **Insl-X Stix**. Standard primers will slide right off these non-porous plastic surfaces.
How long will a professional cabinet paint job last?
If done with a **Urethane Alkyd Enamel** and proper degreasing, you can expect **8 to 10 years** of high-traffic use. Unlike walls, you cannot "patch paint" cabinets easily; once the finish fails, it usually requires a full professional sand-down and respray.
Is it cheaper to paint or reface cabinets?
**Painting is significantly cheaper.** A professional paint job for a medium kitchen averages **$3,000–$5,000**, whereas refacing (replacing just the doors and skins) starts at **$8,000–$12,000**. Painting offers the highest ROI if your current door style is still modern.
Should I brush and roll or spray?
For a factory look, **spraying is mandatory.** Brushing and rolling will always leave "stipple" (orange peel texture) or brush marks, which trap grease and dirt more easily. I recommend rolling the "boxes" in the kitchen and spraying the doors in a controlled garage environment.

Revive the Heart of Your Home

Painting your kitchen cabinets is the single most impactful renovation you can perform on a budget. However, that impact only lasts if the finish is bulletproof. Take the time to degrease, fill the grain, and use a sprayer. Your future self (and your home's resale value) will thank you.
