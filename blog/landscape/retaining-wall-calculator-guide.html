Retaining Wall Calculator: The "Buried" Truth

The Mason's Rules

  • The Buried Course: The bottom row of blocks MUST be buried underground. If your wall is 3 feet high, you are actually buying blocks for a 3.5-foot wall.
  • The "Face Foot" Math: Don't count individual blocks yet. Calculate the total square footage (Face Feet) first, then divide by the block size.
  • Caps are Separate: Wall blocks and Cap stones (the flat top ones) are sold differently. One is by Area, the other is by Linear Feet.

Gravity is relentless. It never sleeps, it never takes a break, and it hates your retaining wall.

The soil behind a 3-foot wall exerts thousands of pounds of pressure. If you just stack some blocks on the grass, that wall will fall over. I guarantee it.

The secret to a wall that stands for 50 years isn't the pretty blocks you see; it's the blocks you bury and the gravel you hide behind them. Today, we're going to calculate the materials for a Segmental Retaining Wall (SRW) correctly—including the "invisible" costs that most DIYers forget until they run out of money.

The Wall Builder's Kit

Building a wall is heavy work. You need tools that force the blocks into place.

  • Dead Blow Hammer (3lb) A metal hammer will crack the blocks. You need a heavy rubber mallet to level them.
  • 4-Foot Box Level If the first row isn't perfectly level, the top row will look like a rollercoaster.
  • Retaining Wall Calculator Calculates blocks, caps, AND the tons of drainage gravel needed for backfill.

Rule #1: The Invisible Row (Embedment)

This is the most common reason DIY walls fail. You buy enough blocks for a 3-foot wall, you stack them 3 feet high on top of the grass, and next winter, the frost heaves the ground and your wall topples over.

The Rule: The bottom row of blocks (the Base Course) must be buried.

// THE EMBEDMENT FORMULA

Bury 1 inch of block for every 1 foot of wall height.

*Minimum embedment is always 6 inches (one full block depth) just to be safe.

The Math Consequence: If you want a 3-foot wall visible , you actually need to build a 3.5-foot wall. You have to buy an entire extra row of blocks that nobody will ever see.

Rule #2: Calculate "Face Feet" (Not Blocks)

Pros don't count blocks one by one. We calculate the total surface area of the wall face.

Step 1: Get Total Area

(Length) × (Total Height including buried row) = Total Face Feet

Step 2: Check Your Block

Most SRW (Segmental Retaining Wall) blocks are not exactly 1 sq. ft.

  • • Large Block: 1.0 sq ft
  • • Medium Block: 0.75 sq ft
  • • Garden Block: 0.33 sq ft
Total Face Feet ÷ Block Size = Total Blocks

Rule #3: Caps are Linear

Don't Forget the "Lid"

The top row of a wall isn't a regular block; it's a solid "Cap Stone" (so dirt doesn't fill the hollow cores).

Math Change: Caps are calculated by Linear Feet, not Area.

Measure the total length of the wall (e.g., 40 feet). If the cap is 18 inches wide (1.5 feet), you need:
40 ÷ 1.5 = 27 Caps.

Rule #4: The Water Killer

Here is a physics fact: Water is heavy. Wet soil is incredibly heavy.

If you stack blocks and backfill the space behind them with dirt (especially clay), when it rains, that dirt turns into heavy mud. It pushes against the wall with massive force (Hydrostatic Pressure). Eventually, the wall bulges and collapses.

The Solution: You must create a "Drainage Zone" of clean stone behind the wall. Water flows through stone instantly, relieving the pressure.

Rule #5: The 12-Inch Gravel Column

Never Backfill with Dirt

Behind every single block, from the bottom to the top (minus the last 6 inches for grass), you need a column of **#57 Stone** (clean 3/4" gravel).

Minimum Width: 12 Inches

Measure 1 foot back from the wall. Fill that entire space with stone. Compact it as you go.

How to Calculate Drainage Gravel

You need way more stone than you think. Here is the math to get the Cubic Feet, which we then convert to Tons.

1
Calculate Volume (Cu. Ft.)

(Wall Length) × (Total Height) × (1 ft Depth) = Total Cubic Feet

Example: 40' Long × 3' High × 1' Deep = 120 Cu. Ft.

2
Convert to Tons

Gravel is dense. One cubic foot weighs about 100-105 lbs.
(Total Cu. Ft.) ÷ 19 = Tons Needed (Approximate)

Example: 120 ÷ 19 = 6.3 Tons of #57 Stone.

Rule #6: The Pipe at the Bottom

4" Perforated Pipe

Place this at the very bottom of your drainage gravel (behind the first buried block). It acts as the emergency exit for water.

Do Not Clog It

Use a "Sock Pipe" (pipe wrapped in fabric) or wrap your gravel column in geotextile fabric. If dirt gets into the pipe, the system fails.

Retaining Wall FAQ: Safety Checks

Do I need Geogrid (Reinforcement Mesh)?
If your wall is over 3 or 4 feet, YES. Geogrid is a high-strength plastic net that you sandwich between the block layers and extend back into the soil. It anchors the wall face to the hill behind it. Without it, tall walls will fall over.
Note: Walls over 4 feet often require an engineer's stamp by law. Check your local code.
Why does the wall lean back? (The Batter)
A good retaining wall is never perfectly vertical. It leans back into the hill (called "Batter") to fight gravity. Most modern SRW blocks have a "lip" or "pin" system that forces this setback automatically (usually about 1 inch back for every course up).
Do I glue the blocks together?
Only the top row (Caps). The weight of the wall and the gravel core holds the main blocks in place (gravity). However, the Cap Stones must be glued down with heavy-duty **SRW Adhesive** so they don't pop off when someone sits on them.

The Verdict

A retaining wall is an iceberg: the most important parts are the ones you don't see. If you try to save money by skipping the buried row or the drainage gravel, you are building a time bomb. Do the math, buy the extra tons of stone, and bury that first course deep. It's cheaper to build it right once than to rebuild it twice.
